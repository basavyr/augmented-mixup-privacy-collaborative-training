================================================================================
Open Tech Days — Competition Form
Singularized Mixup: Privacy-Preserving Collaborative AI, Made in Orange
================================================================================

A demonstration by Orange Research and Orange Services, building on internally
developed research recently published at a leading peer-reviewed venue in
machine learning.

All third-party figures (regulatory data, market sizing, competing schemes)
are traceable to the Sources section at the end of this file.

================================================================================
Q1. In which context does your demonstration topic fit?
================================================================================

Every regulated industry — healthcare, telco, finance, public sector — sits on
data that would produce dramatically better AI models if pooled across silos,
but that cannot legally be shared. GDPR enforcement alone has now reached
EUR 6.1 billion in cumulative fines across 2,888 decisions (May 2026), with
the Media/Telecoms sector accounting for EUR 4.97 billion of that total [S1].
The EU AI Act [S2] and sector-specific frameworks (HIPAA, DORA, national
health-data laws) add further pressure.

The technical answers tried so far are failing in different ways. Federated
learning is operationally heavy and demonstrably vulnerable to gradient-
inversion attacks [S3]. Homomorphic encryption remains orders of magnitude
too slow at deep-learning scale. And the most popular lightweight data-mixing
schemes — InstaHide and NeuraCrypt — were both broken in 2021 by Carlini and
collaborators [S5][S6]. Our demonstration occupies exactly this gap: it
delivers the cost and scalability profile of data-mixing while neutralising
the attacks that killed the previous generation, in a market segment that is
both large and accelerating (Privacy Management Software alone is forecast at
USD 15.2B by 2028 with a 41.9% CAGR [S7]).

================================================================================
Q2. Where is the innovation in your demonstration?
================================================================================

The innovation is at three levels — conceptual, theoretical, and practical —
and that combination is what makes it defensible.

Conceptually, we apply singularization — a principle Orange already uses to
harden cryptography on resource-constrained telecom devices such as SIM
cards [S12] — to the problem of collaborative AI training. Every prior
mixing scheme failed for the same root cause: the same private sample
reappeared across many encoded outputs, leaving a persistent statistical
signal that adversaries could cluster on and unwind. Our method removes
that signal at the source. Each private sample participates in essentially
one mixture, and that mixture is deliberately poisoned with structured
spherical noise on its non-target component. A single, elegant design
choice neutralises the entire attack family that broke the previous
generation of schemes.

Theoretically, we turn this design into a provable security argument with
two original results: a minimax lower bound on the reconstruction error of
any attacker — linear or non-linear, even one with full knowledge of the
mixing weights — and a closed-form calibration rule that sets the injected
noise as a simple multiple of the average inter-sample distance to hit any
chosen privacy target. This is a sharp departure from prior schemes whose
security proofs collapsed once researchers examined them under realistic
data assumptions.

Practically, the resulting protocol is one-shot. Each party transforms its
data once, locally, and ships the result. There is no iterative gradient
exchange, no model-update orchestration, no secure-channel choreography, no
federated-learning infrastructure to stand up and maintain. An entire layer
of operational complexity — and an entire class of known attack surfaces —
simply disappears. The technology is small enough to fit in a few dozen
lines of code, yet powerful enough to change the economics of privacy-
preserving AI.

================================================================================
Q3. What are the technologies used in your demonstration?
================================================================================

The core algorithm is small and self-contained: roughly thirty lines of
PyTorch that perform a random pairing, a convex combination, and an
isotropic spherical-noise injection. It is backed by minimax estimation
theory and directional signal-to-noise analysis. Pretrained ResNet
backbones serve as frozen, publicly available feature extractors; they are
not part of the privacy mechanism, which means the security guarantee does
not depend on any private artefact. A lightweight classifier head is trained
on top of the singularized representations.

To evaluate the security claim honestly, we also implement the attacker's
side: a gradient-based linear inversion attack with total-variation and L2
priors, and a learned non-linear U-Net reconstruction attack trained on
public images. Reconstruction quality is measured with SNR, SSIM, and
perceptual similarity (LPIPS). The federated-learning comparison uses
FedProx as baseline. Benchmarks span MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100,
Tiny-ImageNet, and CIFAR-5M (five million images). The entire pipeline runs
on a single commodity GPU — no specialised cryptographic accelerator, no
secure multi-party computation cluster.

================================================================================
Q4. What is differentiating / remarkable in your demonstration?
================================================================================

The headline differentiator is that our method resists the very attacks that
killed the prior generation. Linear inversion (Carlini-style), Gram-matrix
attacks (Chen-style), fusion-denoising attacks (Luo-style), and a state-of-
the-art learned U-Net attacker all fail once the privacy parameter crosses a
modest threshold: reconstructions degrade into pure noise and become
visually unrecognisable. Crucially, this holds under a deliberately
conservative threat model in which the attacker is given full knowledge of
the mixing weights — a level of generosity to the adversary that most prior
schemes never granted.

The second differentiator is that this strong privacy posture costs almost
nothing in utility. Even at a privacy setting two orders of magnitude
stricter than required to defeat the attacks, our method matches or beats
the strongest published configuration of InstaHide across MNIST, CIFAR-10
and CIFAR-100, with a measurable improvement of nearly two percentage points
on CIFAR-100. The third differentiator is that, in collaborative training
across many parties with heterogeneous data, our method outperforms the
federated-learning baseline by up to twelve percentage points — while being
a one-shot protocol that structurally eliminates the gradient-inversion
attack surface that affects every iterative federated deployment [S3].

================================================================================
Q5. What concrete need / customer benefit does your demonstration address?
================================================================================

We address a pain point that is universal across regulated industries: the
data we would need to train the next generation of AI models cannot legally
be shared across silos. For data-science teams, our demonstration unlocks
training on the effective union of partner datasets without the long legal
and DPO negotiation cycle that today blocks most cross-silo projects. For
DPOs and compliance officers, it changes the conversation from "we hope the
data is safe" to "no attacker — linear or non-linear — can recover the
original samples below a mathematically proven error bound."

The demo brings this to life in a healthcare scenario. A consortium of
European hospitals wants to train a diagnostic model for a rare condition.
Sharing scans is blocked by GDPR [S10] and national health-data law;
federated learning has proven fragile and leaks through gradients [S3];
homomorphic encryption is too slow. With our approach, each hospital
transforms its scans once, locally; a central institute trains one model
on the union; no individual scan can be recovered by anyone; and diagnostic
accuracy matches what a fully centralised model would have achieved. The
same pattern transposes directly to telco fraud detection across operators,
AML across banks, and threat-intelligence sharing across enterprises.

Q6. Can you make the link between your demonstration and the innovation
    strategy of Orange?
================================================================================

The fit is genuinely strong across multiple strategic vectors. This is a
home-grown Orange innovation, invented inside Orange Research and Orange
Services and building directly on prior Orange work in singularization for
telecom security [S12]. It reinforces Orange's positioning on trust, digital
sovereignty, and responsible AI [S11][S13] by providing something the market
currently lacks: a quantifiable privacy guarantee that can be put in front
of regulators, auditors, and customers.

It is also directly monetisable through existing Orange channels. It can
ship as a Privacy-Enhancing Technology (PET) feature inside Orange Business
data-collaboration offerings, competing head-to-head with AWS Clean Rooms
[S14] and Snowflake Data Clean Rooms [S15]. It can power
threat-intelligence-sharing platforms inside Orange Cyberdefense. It can
serve as a building block for Orange's healthcare and eHealth initiatives.
And internally, it unlocks cross-affiliate analytics that today stall on
intra-group data-flow constraints. Peer-reviewed publication of the
underlying research gives Orange visible thought leadership in a domain
otherwise dominated by Google, Apple, and Meta.

================================================================================
Q7. What is the business potential of your demonstration?
================================================================================

The business potential can be assessed along three time horizons. In the
short term (6-12 months), the primary value is internal: enabling specific
cross-affiliate analytics use cases that are currently blocked or delayed
by data-protection constraints. Plausible candidates include fraud-signal
sharing between country operators, joint analytics on customer-experience
data, and network-anomaly correlation across affiliates. The expected
benefit is a reduction in the time and effort required to obtain legal
and DPO approval for such projects, together with a clearer compliance
narrative supported by quantitative privacy guarantees.

In the medium term (12-24 months), the technology can be integrated as a
component of existing Orange offers, subject to product-team validation:
as a building block within Orange Business data-collaboration products,
as an enabler for threat-intelligence-sharing scenarios in Orange
Cyberdefense, and as a candidate technology for multi-institution
training in Orange healthcare and eHealth initiatives. In the long term
(24-48 months), and conditional on demonstrated traction in the previous
phases, the underlying method could support a standalone Privacy-Enhancing
Technology offering or licensing arrangements with adjacent vendors in
the PET ecosystem (for example Duality, Decentriq, or TripleBlind).
Realising each horizon will require dedicated product, legal, and
go-to-market work beyond the scope of the research itself.

The addressable market is large and accelerating. Privacy Management
Software is forecast at USD 15.2B by 2028 with a 41.9% CAGR [S7]; the
broader enterprise governance, risk and compliance market reaches
USD 39.99B by 2030 [S8]; and Privacy-Enhancing Computation has been named
a top strategic technology trend by Gartner [S9]. Defensibility comes from
three reinforcing sources: a peer-reviewed publication anchoring the
science, a head-to-head empirical story against InstaHide, federated
learning, and homomorphic encryption, and a first-mover position in the
trust vacuum that opened when the previous generation of mixing schemes was
publicly broken [S5][S6].

================================================================================
Q8. Do you have another important point to report?
================================================================================

Two points are worth emphasising. First, the timing is exceptional. The two
most-cited learnable-obfuscation schemes were broken within months of each
other [S5][S6], leaving a vacuum in the privacy-preserving ML landscape with
very few credible candidates to fill it. Orange has both the research asset
and the industrial reach to occupy that vacuum now, rather than catching up
later. Second, the technology is production-ready in principle. The core
mechanism is a few dozen lines of PyTorch; it runs on commodity hardware;
it integrates with any downstream training pipeline; and the reference
implementation is openly available for reproduction and audit.

A note of intellectual honesty for the jury: our security guarantee is not
cryptographic in the strict formal sense — we do not claim epsilon-delta
differential privacy or semantic security. What we provide is an
average-case reconstruction lower bound and directional signal-to-noise
control, validated empirically against the strongest known attacks. This
is precisely the right trade-off for the privacy / utility / cost regime
where federated learning and homomorphic encryption both fail in practice.
Being transparent about it is itself a differentiator in a field where
overclaiming has repeatedly damaged credibility.
